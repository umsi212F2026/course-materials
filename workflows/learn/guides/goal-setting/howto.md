# Goal setting: your side of the conversation

Before you learn something, you and an agent spend a few minutes writing down what you want to
be able to do and what would count as having got there. It goes in a file called `goals.md`.
The agent has its own instructions; this is about yours.

## Why do this first

**Goals keep you going when the work gets tedious.** Most learning has a stretch in the middle
that is dull or hard or both. Knowing exactly what you're going to be able to do at the end of
it motivates you across that stretch.

**It stops you learning the wrong things.** Fields are much larger than any particular use of
them. Deciding up front what you actually need is how you avoid spending three hours on the
part you were never going to touch.

**It lets the agent pick better activities — or build them.** An agent that knows you need to
_spot a broken one_ rather than _make one from scratch_ will suggest different work, and can
make it for you: a set of examples to sort, a deliberately flawed thing to find the flaw in, a
practice case that doesn't exist anywhere yet. Vague goals get you a lecture. Specific goals
get you something to do.

**It gives you a stopping point.** Without one you either quit at the first moment you feel
vaguely competent, or you keep going long past the point of use.

## What to expect

A few minutes, and a handful of questions. The agent has been told not to explain any of the
subject during this conversation — if you ask, it'll say it will explain later. That's not
being difficult; it's the point.

The file stays open in front of both of you. Watch it get filled in, or get in there and make
edits yourself.

**One thing not to edit: the short identifier at the top of each goal** — `c-read-diagram`,
`w-schema`. It looks like clutter and it isn't. Everything you ever do gets recorded against
that identifier, so renaming one after you've done some work orphans the record: the work stays
in the log, and the goal reads as though you'd never started it. Rewrite the wording all you
like; leave the identifier alone. If a goal turns into a genuinely different goal, that's a new
goal, and the agent will make you one.

## Your job

Be honest. If you don't know something, or you are uncertain about your knowledge, say so. If
your only motivation (so far) is to pass a quiz in the course, or to avoid sounding foolish
during in-class explanation exercises, you can say so. If you're not sure yet what would be a
good behavioral test of whether you've mastered the topic, say so. The agent may be able to
make suggestions.

Be critical. Don't assume that the agent understood you, or that it has good judgment about
what reasonable learning goals are or reasonable ways of assessing whether you've met those
goals. A person might say, "Wait, I couldn't follow that." An agent is more likely to just make
the best sense it can and run with it. So you can't assume that whatever it fills in captures
what you really intend; you'll have to read it and edit it.

## Moves for a goal-setting conversation

All of [Moves for any conversation with a thought partner](../thought-partner-moves.md) apply
here. Below are the ones particular to setting goals.

**"Is that too broad?"** Check on whether the thing you say you want to learn is actually a
multi-month project.

**"That's more than I need."** Agents are thorough by default and will happily spec you a
semester of things to learn when you only want a week's worth.

**"Show me what passing and failing look like."** If an assessment criterion is real, the agent
can describe a version of your work that meets it and a version that doesn't. If it can't
produce the failing version, the criterion isn't a criterion.

**"What would this miss?"** Ask what you might still be unable to do even if you met all the
goals.

**"That's enough."** You decide when this is over. The agent (and you) will always be able to
point out ways in which your goals document could be improved. Just make it good enough to
guide the next two phases of the workflow.
