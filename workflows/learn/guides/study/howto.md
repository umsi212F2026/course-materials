# Study sessions: your side

This is where nearly all the time goes. [The overall guide](../learn/howto.md) covers how
sessions fit together; this is what happens inside one.

## The shape of a session

The tutor says where things stand, you pick something to work on from a few options, you do it,
it gets recorded, and you pick again — until you stop. You stop whenever you like.

What the tutor suggests next comes from the topic's sequence: sets of goals your goals file
puts in an order, so that the words come before the things that use them. Within a set it picks
for you. It is only a suggestion, so you can always pick something else, from any set, and
nobody comments.

**The progress view.** At the start the tutor shows a picture of the topic: one row for each set
of goals, in order, with a mark for each goal. `#` is met, `~` is in progress but not met yet, `.` is
not started, and `>` is one you said you will learn elsewhere. The number at the end of a row is
how many are met out of how many. An arrow usually points at the set the tutor will suggest from
next (it skips goals with nothing ready to study, so now and then it suggests from a later set),
and below the picture it names what is left there. After each attempt you see just that set's
line, so you can watch the marks change; when you finish a set you see the whole picture again.

The options come with a note on what's different about each, not just titles. If two sound the
same to you, say so; the tutor may be able to explain and, if not, it's a real problem that
should be sent back to the curator or the goal-setting process.

## Two kinds of thing you'll do

**Studying something** — reading, watching, working through a solved example. With one
difference from how you'd normally do it: there's a conversation running alongside.

**Attempting something** — doing a task your goals said you wanted to be able to do.

**Words** turn up throughout and work a little differently — no choice is offered, the tutor
just sets one of six short tasks. See [the vocabulary guide](../vocabulary/howto.md).

## The conversation while you read something or watch a video.

You say what you think something means. The tutor comes back with an awkward case — "you said
X; does it still count if…" — rather than telling you whether you're right.

This will occasionally be irritating. It's the only cheap way to tell a real grasp from a
sentence that sounded right, and reading your own explanation back is how you find out which
one you have.

**Try going first.** Tell the tutor agent what you think something means and ask it if you have
it right.

**If not, go second!** If you ask the tutor to explain something, don't just read what it says.
Check your understanding by paraphrasing, or drawing out some implication ("Is that just
another word for...?")

With a text passage or image that the tutor can also ingest, it may also ask you about things
you didn't flag. Those are usually terms the text used before defining, or steps in an example
nobody explained.

## Asking for help

**Ask whenever you want it.** Help is free and there's no penalty.

If you do get through a task without help, the tutor quietly sends a transcript to an
independent agent that checks what you did for correctness. It then logs the attempt.

## When the checker says not yet

The checker is deliberately tough. When it says you haven't met the goal, you'll see its
feedback and three choices:

1. **Try again now**, on another question from the same activity.
2. **Come back to it later.**
3. **Mark it as learned.** Your call, and nobody asks you to justify it. It counts as met, and it
   comes back in a review a few days later, checked just as strictly. So you aren't skipping the
   test, just moving it.

## Goals you'll learn in class

Some goals are also taught in class or on a problem set. When the tutor first reaches one, it
says what the goal is, what would count, and where it is taught, then offers four choices:

1. **Do it here.** Ordinary study.
2. **Already done elsewhere.** Say where, if you like. It counts as met, and it comes back in a
   review a few days later, checked just as strictly.
3. **I'll learn it there later.** Say where. The tutor stops offering it.
4. **Remove it.** The goal is dropped, in your words.

You can say you'll pick any goal up elsewhere at any time, marked or not. Once everything else
is met, the tutor asks once about each deferred goal: is it done there? "Yes" counts it as met.
"Not yet" leaves it as it is. "Let's do it here" brings it back.

## Notes

`notes.md` is yours. You write it; the tutor might prompt when something was hard, and can type
what you said, but the words should be yours.

Write it for **you in six weeks**, who will have forgotten most of this and needs to get back
up to speed in three minutes. That's a real audience and it's a demanding one. A note in the
agent's voice will be no use to the future you. Really long notes will also be of no use.

Keep it revised rather than accumulated. When your understanding changes, replace the old
version instead of adding a correction underneath — it's a statement of what you think now, not
a history of what you've thought.

The template has five sections, and each is there for a reason:

**Glossary, in your own words.** Not copied. A term you can define from the source but not in
your own words isn't one you have yet. If you write these in your own words, you can ask the
tutor to check them for you, which will be valuable feedback.

**What clicked.** A reminder of how it started to make sense to you can help you see it again
in the future.

**What you got wrong.** When you come at it fresh, you might make the same mistake again; you
can preempt that with a note.

**One example you understand completely.** A concrete case can get you back in faster than any
definition will.

**Deliberately skipped.** What you decided not to learn, and why. Stops you reopening a scoping
argument with yourself, and tells you what to pick up if what you need changes.

None of it is required, and an empty section beats a padded one.

## Things to say

All the [thought partner moves](../thought-partner-moves.md) apply. Here are some particular to
a study session:

**"Let me try first."** The tutor should wait. If it's jumping in before you're stuck, say so.

**"Just tell me."** Sometimes the right call. You're not obliged to discover everything.

**"This isn't working."** About an activity — the reading is impenetrable, the task seems to be
testing something else. Say it. But be honest with yourself about whether it's a bad activity
or a hard one; the hard ones are where the learning is, and an exit will always be available if
you want one.

**"I want to stick with this."** If the tutor suggests dropping something and you disagree, you
win. It's your time.

**"What am I actually being asked to do here?"** If an activity's instructions are vague, the
entry is at fault, not you. Ask.

**"I want to change the goals."** This is where it usually happens — you're part-way into
attempting something and realize the target was wrong. It's one of the more useful things that
can happen.

Expect one question first: is it the goal that's wrong, or just this activity? Worth answering
honestly, because they need different fixes — but one question, and then you're taken at your
word. Changing the goals is its own conversation, so this session will be wound up first.

**"What would I have to do to be finished with this?"** Always answerable, and worth asking
early.

**"I've got this. Mark it learned."** Any time, not only after the checker says not yet.
